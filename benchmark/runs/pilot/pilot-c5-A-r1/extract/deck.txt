=== Slide 2 ===
[Title] Recommendation: do not launch Parcelbird Now in 2027. Put the money into the software business, which can break even within today's cash if growth holds
- EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
- 1
- Orders lose money at every level of performance we have seen.
- At the median of the 14 customer sites our software runs, each order loses $2.67 before dark-store costs and $6.17 after them. Combining the best value seen at any site on every input, which no single site has managed, still loses $1.76.
- Decisions we ask the board to take
- 1.
- Decline Parcelbird Now for 2027.
- 2
- The founders' plan turns positive only by beating our best site on three drivers at once.
- It assumes a $45 order (best seen: $42), 3.5 orders per rider-hour (best: 3.0), 1,800 orders a day (best: 1,400) and promotions at 2% of order value (every site: 6%). If order value or rider productivity only reaches the median, orders lose money again.
- 2.
- Approve $0.8M a year for 2 engineers and 2 sales hires to work the 37-grocer pipeline.
- 3.
- Ask management to package the software for grocers who want fast delivery from their own stores, selling to that trend rather than competing with our pipeline.
- 3
- Our own test and the market point the same way.
- The Columbus pop-up lost about $12.57 per order, falling below the median on every driver, and only 22% of customers were still ordering in month three. Both incumbents lose money on every order; QuickBasket closed 4 of its 11 cities in 2025.
- 4.
- Agree now what would reopen the question (page 12).
- 4
- The plan breaks the 12-month runway rule even if it works; the software path does not.
- Holding 12 months of runway leaves $7.2M to deploy, and the plan needs $9.5M. Adding $0.8M a year of engineering and sales reaches break-even in months 26–32 if growth holds at 45–60% and other costs stay flat.
- Source: Parcelbird board briefing pack; Parcelbird analysis.

=== Slide 14 ===
[Title] How we handled gaps in the briefing pack, and what management should confirm
- APPENDIX B: METHOD AND OPEN QUESTIONS
- Choices made in this analysis
- Open questions for management
- Two contribution measures. “Before store costs” covers every per-order cost; “after store costs” also spreads each dark store's fixed cost over its orders. The pack does not define the founders' or incumbents' “contribution per order”, so we show both.
- The $9.5M plan figure. The pack does not say whether it includes today's $0.4M a month burn, so page 9 shows both readings. The conclusion holds under either.
- Runway = cash ÷ current monthly burn. We test the reserve at today's burn, the most lenient basis.
- Timing. The $12.0M is treated as cash available in January 2027; the pack does not date it.
- Software path (page 11) is a top-down illustration with stated assumptions, not a forecast.
- Pop-up exit rate of about 410 orders a day assumes steady 18% weekly growth across the six weeks (11,800 orders in total).
- Waterfall (page 6) is sequential. A different order changes the step sizes, not the end points.
- How was 22% month-three retention measured in a six-week test?
- Does the $9.5M include today's software burn, and what ramp from launch to 1,800 orders a day does it assume?
- Is there evidence from any operator, beyond our 14 sites, of 1,800 orders a day or 3.5 orders per rider-hour?
- Which minimum-pay proposals are under discussion, at what rate, and in which two cities?
- Which pipeline grocers would see Parcelbird Now as a competitor, and how much ARR do they represent?
- Source: Parcelbird board briefing pack. The pack labels its company, competitor and market figures as synthetic benchmark data. No external research was used.
