## Part 1 · Normalised storyline

**Governing thought:** Keep buying housings in 2027 and spend on supply resilience, not machinery — the owned cell breaks the board's limits and the lease's edge is too thin to bet on

**Executive summary:**
1. The owned cell is not a 2027 option. It needs $7.3M against the $5.0M capital limit and would leave ~$3.2M of year-end cash, below the $4.0M floor. The circulated “$27 saving” is $16 per good housing in 2027 once freight, scrap and fixed cost are counted; payback is 3.7 years at 100k units (7.2 at 65k), not “under three”.
2. Leasing is a cost near-tie that turns negative if any one assumption slips. Over 2027–29 the lease saves $0.3M (1%) versus buying in the planning case, but costs $1.4M more in the conservative demand case and $0.5M more if it runs only seven months of 2027. It needs ≥207k units in 2028–29 just to break even.
3. Delivery reliability is a supply-design problem that buying can fix faster than any cell. The 0.2-point shortfall (96.8% vs 97% on time) traces to one flood closure. Buffer stock, a tooled alternate shop, 40k reserved units and an emergency supplier act in 0–13 weeks — a cell needs 5–9+ months — for $0.25M of capital and up to $0.72M of working capital.
4. Pre-agreed gates would switch the answer to the lease; act before the March 2027 quote expiry. Trigger the lease if 2028–29 volume is firmly committed at ≥208k units, the supplier's 2028–29 delivered price exceeds ~$84 (or ~$74 with committed volume), or on-time delivery stays below 97% for two quarters. The plan keeps 2027 year-end cash near $8.2M.
