## Part 1 · Normalised storyline

**Governing thought:** Recommendation: do not launch Parcelbird Now in 2027. Put the money into the software business instead

**Executive summary:**
1. Our own data says the orders lose money. At the median of the 14 dark stores our software already runs, each order loses $6.17 after store costs ($2.67 before them). Even the best observed value for every input, combined, still loses $1.76. No site has ever achieved that combination.
2. The plan needs four records broken at once. The founders' +$2.02 per order needs a $45 average order, 3.5 orders per rider-hour, 1,800 orders a day and promotions at 2%. Each is beyond anything a site has achieved. The Columbus pop-up fell short of the benchmark median on all four, losing $12.57 per order.
3. It breaks the cash rule even if it works. Spending $9.5M of $12.0M leaves $2.5M at month 18. That is about 6 months of runway at today's burn, half the 12-month floor. At median economics, nine stores would lose ~$18M a year, and a new round is 'unlikely'.
4. Software is the cheaper way to catch the same growth. Nine late-stage deals are worth $0.86M ARR (+21%), at 81% gross margin, for $0.8M a year of hires. Pipeline grocers want fast delivery from their own stores, which is something we can sell them rather than compete with.
5. Decisions asked of the board: (1) decline Parcelbird Now as proposed; (2) approve ~$0.8M a year for software hiring; (3) agree the evidence that would reopen the operator question (p. 12).

- Pipeline values = deals × $96,000 average contract; close rates are not in the pack, so the full pipeline is an upper bound.
- Nine-store results are steady state, before launch marketing and head-office cost.
- Bridge applies plan inputs one at a time in the order shown; steps may not sum exactly due to rounding.
- Best observed values for each input came from different sites; no site has combined them.
- Density in the launch cities is the biggest open question (§12); whether customers keep ordering at higher prices is open.
- Two of the three launch cities are considering minimum pay standards for delivery workers; not costed in the plan.
- The lead investor calls a new round for a delivery operator 'unlikely in this market'.

=== Slide 14 ===
[Title] Appendix B: judgment calls and limits of this analysis
- APPENDIX
- •
- Definition of contribution. The pack does not define the founders' 'contribution profit'. We show results both before and after the dark-store fixed cost. The conclusion holds either way: at the median an order loses money even before the store is paid for.
- •
- Cash need. We read the $9.5M as the total company need to month 18, which is the most favourable reading for the plan. If it is on top of today's $0.4M a month burn, the plan needs ~$16.7M, more than the $12.0M in the bank.
- •
- Pop-up ramp. Run-rates of ~180 and ~410 orders a day assume steady 18% weekly growth over six weeks. The pop-up was a temporary site with a lower $650k fixed cost, so it is evidence on demand and operations, not on mature-store economics.
- •
- Customer payback. Payback uses contribution after store cost per order and leaves out orders in months 1–2 and churn after month 3, because the pack has no data on either.
- •
- Zipcart comparison. We apply Zipcart's fee, promotion and volume changes to our benchmarks with every other input unchanged. Zipcart's own definition of contribution is not given.
- •
- Not modelled. Launch marketing, head-office cost and ramp-up losses (all inside the founders' $9.5M), and close rates on the software pipeline, which the pack does not give.
- Source: Board briefing pack; Parcelbird analysis.
